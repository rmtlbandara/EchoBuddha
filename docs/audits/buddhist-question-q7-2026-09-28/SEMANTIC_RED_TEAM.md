## Claim-level attacks

- **“The Met object is the first Buddha statue”:** rejected. It is a securely dated example, not proof of the absolute first object.
- **“No surviving lifetime statue means none existed”:** rejected. The page distinguishes missing evidence from proof of nonexistence.
- **“Early symbolic art proves an image ban”:** rejected. Visible form does not establish one motive or universal rule.
- **“Xuanzang witnessed King Udayana’s commission”:** rejected. Xuanzang records the story in the seventh century, roughly a millennium after the period described.
- **“The Udayana story has no value because it is late”:** rejected. It is treated as important evidence for later Buddhist tradition, not discarded.
- **“The MN 142 passage proves a lifetime statue”:** rejected. Its setting is explicitly after Parinibbāna.
- **“The MN 115 passage dates the first image”:** rejected. It shows commentarial familiarity with images but supplies no origin date.
- **“Buddhaghosa invented every commentarial sentence in the fifth century”:** rejected. Earlier Sinhala material is acknowledged, while the date of each inherited passage remains uncertain.

## Attribution attacks

- The relic-bearing-image passage is verified in the *Dakkhiṇāvibhaṅgasuttavaṇṇanā*, commentary to MN 142; it is not attributed to the *Dhātuvibhaṅga* commentary.
- The Bodhi-branch passage is verified in the *Bahudhātukasuttavaṇṇanā*, commentary to MN 115; it is not attributed to *Manorathapūraṇī*.
- Xuanzang is identified as a historical Buddhist pilgrim whose travel record preserves a tradition, not an early Buddhist discourse or eyewitness testimony.
- Museum evidence supports chronology; it is not treated as doctrinal authority.
