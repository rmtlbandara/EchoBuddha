## Reader and intent

The target reader knows that suttas are Buddhist texts but feels unsure when opening one: the collection abbreviations are unfamiliar, the repetition feels strange, translation wording differs, and it is not obvious how to move from ancient setting to responsible daily practice.

Primary intent: learn a practical, source-aware method for reading one Buddhist sutta.

Secondary intent: understand reference numbers, translation/paraphrase boundaries, oral-style repetition, and how to choose a manageable starting text.

This page does not own:

- the definition of “sutta” (`/learn/buddhist-dictionary/sutta/`);
- the definition and scope of the Pali Canon (`/learn/buddhist-dictionary/pali-canon/`);
- the meaning of a named discourse (existing source-study pages);
- a universal reading method for every Buddhist canon or tradition.

## Unique promise

Give the reader a reusable three-pass method:

1. Orient: identify collection, speaker, audience, setting, and question.
2. Trace: notice the structure, lists, similes, repeated formulas, and the movement of the teaching.
3. Apply carefully: separate translation from commentary, compare a key passage if wording matters, and carry one bounded practice question into the day.

Include one worked example using `SN 56.11` only to demonstrate how a reference and structure are read. Do not reproduce a translation or compete with the existing SN 56.11 source-study page.

- Say “sutta” for the Pali form and note that Sanskrit/Buddhist contexts may use “sutra.”

## Outline

1. Study focus: a sutta is read as a situated teaching, not a detached quote.
2. What to know before opening the text.
3. Decode common references: DN, MN, SN, AN; mention that other collections use other systems.
4. First pass - orient to speaker, audience, setting, and problem.
5. Second pass - trace lists, contrasts, similes, repetition, and conclusion.
6. Third pass - compare wording where necessary and form one practice question.
7. Worked example: reading the shape of SN 56.11 without reproducing its translation.
8. Common mistakes: quote-mining, treating summary as translation, universalizing one tradition, forcing immediate agreement, collecting without practice.
9. A seven-question reading card.
10. Where to continue on Echo Buddha.
